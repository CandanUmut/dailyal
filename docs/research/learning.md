# Learning methods: what the research really shows

> Research phase R0, 2026-11-20. For each claim I found the paper and read at least its abstract. "Abstract only" means I did not read the full paper. "Second-hand" means I could not open the paper and rely on how other papers describe it. Almost all studies are on English or other European languages, not Arabic.

## Summary

**Holds up:** retrieval practice; spacing (spread beats crammed); topic information before listening; text on screen helping L2 listening; multi-word phrases being processed faster; desirable difficulties as a general idea.

**Overstated in PLAN §2:**
- Coverage figures (95%, 98%) are targets, not sharp lines, and come from English.
- Growing gaps (1, 2, 4, 8 …) do **not** beat equal gaps for long-term memory.
- Narrow listening across days is untested; repeating within one sitting is supported.
- Roots: I found no study showing root teaching speeds adult vocabulary growth.
- "Dual coding" is the wrong label: audio and text are both words.
- Bjork gives no target miss rate.

**Change in the app:** label doubling gaps, the 5–15% miss rate and text fading as design choices. Add listening steps (predict, listen, check, listen again). Keep comparing self-report with checks.

## 1. Lexical coverage

**Sources**
- Laufer, B. (1989). What percentage of text-lexis is essential for comprehension? In C. Laurén & M. Nordman (Eds.), *Special language: From humans thinking to thinking machines* (pp. 316–323). Multilingual Matters. *Second-hand.*
- Hu, M., & Nation, P. (2000). Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension. *Reading in a Foreign Language, 13*(1), 403–430. https://hdl.handle.net/10125/66973. *Record and summaries only.*
- Nation, I. S. P. (2006). How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening? *Canadian Modern Language Review, 63*(1), 59–82. https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59. *Abstract only.*
- van Zeeland, H., & Schmitt, N. (2013). Lexical coverage in L1 and L2 listening comprehension: The same or different from reading comprehension? *Applied Linguistics, 34*(4), 457–479. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ams074. *Abstract only.*
- Checks: Schmitt, Cobb, Horst & Schmitt (2017), *Language Teaching, 50*(2), 212–226 (summary page read); Kremmel et al. (2023), *Language Learning, 73*(4), 1127–1163, https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622 (abstract).

**What it shows.** Laufer: more learners passed a reading test at 95% known words. Hu & Nation (66 learners): about 98% for unassisted reading. Nation: *if* 98% is needed, English needs 8,000–9,000 word families for reading, 6,000–7,000 for listening. Van Zeeland & Schmitt: most listeners followed short informal stories (heard more than once) at 90%, but learners varied a lot; 95% gave steadier results. Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011, via Schmitt et al. 2017) found a straight line, not a cliff. Kremmel et al. could not fully replicate Hu & Nation.

**Verdict: VERIFIED as cited, PARTLY as used.** The numbers are real. They are not thresholds, and they are for English word families.

**For the app.** Use 95% as a target for choosing Listen passages, not a promise that understanding starts there. Nation 2006 is about vocabulary *size*, not teaching order; for "frequency first" our own count (`frequency.md`) is the evidence.

## 2. Retrieval practice

**Source.** Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science, 319*(5865), 966–968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408. *Abstract only.*

**What it shows.** Students learned Swahili–English word pairs. After a word was recalled once, more studying added nothing on a delayed test; more testing gave a large gain. Students' predictions of their own scores did not match their results.

**Verdict: VERIFIED.**

**For the app.** Keep "recall first, then reveal". Keep testing a word after the first correct answer.

## 3. Spacing, and growing versus equal gaps

**Sources** (abstracts only)
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(3), 354–380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
- Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2007). *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 33*(4), 704–719. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704
- Kang, S. H. K., Lindsey, R. V., Mozer, M. C., & Pashler, H. (2014). *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 21*(6), 1544–1550. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0636-z
- Nakata, T. (2015). *Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 37*(4), 677–711. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825
- Latimier, A., Peyre, H., & Ramus, F. (2021). *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(3), 959–987. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8. *Second-hand* (numbers from summaries of the abstract).

**What it shows.** Cepeda (317 experiments): spaced beats massed; the best gap grows with how long you must remember. Karpicke & Roediger: growing gaps won at 10 minutes, equal gaps at 2 days; delaying the *first* test mattered most. Kang: same recall 8 weeks later, but growing gaps gave more correct answers *during* training. Nakata: a small edge for growing gaps; the amount of spacing mattered more. Latimier: spaced vs massed g = 0.74; growing vs equal g = 0.03 (none).

**Verdict: spacing VERIFIED. "Growing gaps are better": NOT SUPPORTED** for long-term memory; one real plus is fewer misses while learning.

**For the app.** Keep doubling as a design choice, and say why: fewer misses, not better memory.

## 4. Topic before listening

**Source.** Chang, A. C.-S., & Read, J. (2006). The effects of listening support on the listening performance of EFL learners. *TESOL Quarterly, 40*(2), 375–397. https://doi.org/10.2307/40264527. *Abstract only.*

**What it shows.** 160 Taiwanese college students. Topic information helped most, then repetition. Vocabulary teaching just before listening helped least at every level.

**Verdict: VERIFIED.** One test setting, English, one sitting.

**For the app.** Context cards before Listen are supported. This does not mean vocabulary is unimportant (see §1).

## 5. Repeated and narrow listening

**Sources.** Chang & Read (2006), above. Krashen, S. (1996). The case for narrow listening. *System, 24*(1), 97–100. https://doi.org/10.1016/0346-251X(95)00054-N (*second-hand*; an argument piece, no experiment). Uchihara, T., Webb, S., & Yanagisawa, A. (2019). *Language Learning, 69*(3), 559–599. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343 (abstract).

**What it shows.** Hearing the input again helped comprehension (second-best support in Chang & Read). Krashen argues for many recordings on one topic but tests nothing. Uchihara: more meetings with a word, more learning (r = .34).

**Verdict: PARTLY.** Repetition in one sitting is supported. Repetition across days and "related passages" are reasonable but untested.

**For the app.** Keep it, labelled as design.

## 6. Roots and morphology in Arabic

**Sources** (abstracts only)
- Tibi, S., & Kirby, J. R. (2017). Morphological awareness: Construct and predictive validity in Arabic. *Applied Psycholinguistics, 38*(5), 1019–1043. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716417000029
- Freynik, S., Gor, K., & O'Rourke, P. (2017). L2 processing of Arabic derivational morphology. *The Mental Lexicon, 12*(1), 21–50. https://doi.org/10.1075/ml.12.1.02fre

**What it shows.** Tibi & Kirby: in 102 Grade 3 children, morphological awareness explained about 48% of the differences in word reading. Freynik et al.: English-speaking learners of Arabic recognise a word faster after one with the same root, beyond shared meaning or sound, much like native speakers.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** Roots are real units for learners too. Children's results are about reading, not vocabulary, and are correlations. I found no study showing that teaching roots speeds adult vocabulary growth.

**For the app.** Keep root groups as a help. Do not claim they speed learning.

## 7. Multi-word chunks

**Sources** (abstracts only). Conklin, K., & Schmitt, N. (2008). *Applied Linguistics, 29*(1), 72–89. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amm022. Yeldham, M. (2020). Does the presence of formulaic language help or hinder second language listeners' lower-level processing? *Language Teaching Research, 24*(3), 338–363. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818787828

**What it shows.** Natives and learners read fixed phrases faster than matched normal phrases. In listening, learners caught words inside fixed phrases more accurately; the gain varied with text difficulty and level. Yeldham notes fixed phrases are often said in reduced form.

**Verdict: VERIFIED** (English data).

**For the app.** Phrase cards are supported. Teach them from audio too.

## 8. Text with audio, then without

**Sources**
- Adesope, O. O., & Nesbit, J. C. (2012). Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 104*(1), 250–263. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147. *Abstract only.*
- Montero Perez, M., Van Den Noortgate, W., & Desmet, P. (2013). Captioned video for L2 listening and vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis. *System, 41*(3), 720–739. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2013.07.013. *Second-hand; I could not get exact numbers.*
- Winke, P., Gass, S., & Sydorenko, T. (2010). The effects of captioning videos used for foreign language listening activities. *Language Learning & Technology, 14*(1), 65–86. *Abstract only.*
- Mayer's redundancy principle and Paivio's dual coding: *not read.*

**What it shows.** Adesope & Nesbit (57 studies): speech plus text beat speech alone, mainly for beginners and fixed-pace material; key words beat full text. Montero Perez: captions had a large effect on listening comprehension and vocabulary. Winke (includes Arabic learners): captions beat none; for Arabic, a *trend* favoured captions on the second viewing; learners called captions a crutch.

**Verdict: PARTLY.** Text helps understanding while it is there. "Dual coding" is the wrong name: Paivio's theory pairs words with images. I found no direct test that fading text builds listening without text.

**For the app.** Rename to "audio with text, then without". Consider: listen once without text, then with text, then without. That changes behaviour, so ask Umut.

## 9. Desirable difficulties

**Sources.** Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. In J. Metcalfe & A. Shimamura (Eds.), *Metacognition* (pp. 185–205). MIT Press (*not read*). Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011). Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way. In M. A. Gernsbacher et al. (Eds.), *Psychology and the real world* (pp. 56–64). Worth. https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf (*full chapter read*).

**What it shows.** Named difficulties: varying conditions, interleaving, spacing, testing. They become undesirable if "the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully". No number is given.

**Verdict: VERIFIED** for the idea. The 5–15% miss rate is ours.

**For the app.** Mix card types and passages. Varying reciters fits "varying conditions" (ask Umut).

## 10. Missing from the plan

1. **Teaching how to listen.** Vandergrift, L., & Tafaghodtari, M. H. (2010). *Language Learning, 60*(2), 470–497. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00559.x (abstract). 106 learners of French: those guided to predict, monitor, evaluate and fix problems beat a group hearing the same texts equally often; weaker listeners gained most. *App:* predict from the context card, listen, note what you got, listen again for gaps.
2. **Self-judgement is unreliable.** Karpicke & Roediger (2008), above; Ross, S. (1998). *Language Testing, 15*(1), 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1177/026553229801500101 (abstract): self-assessment accuracy varies and depends on experience with the skill. *App:* keep self-report next to checks; spot-check the "know / not sure" sweep.
3. **Cards versus listening for new words.** Webb, S., Yanagisawa, A., & Uchihara, T. (2020). *Modern Language Journal, 104*(4), 715–738. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671; Webb, S., Uchihara, T., & Yanagisawa, A. (2023). *Language Teaching, 56*(2), 161–180. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507 (abstracts). Word-focused work: about 39% of words recalled later. Listening alone: about 13–15%. *App:* cards stay the main word engine; Listen builds understanding.
